African St Laurent, Warped

One authored marble slab, shown four ways — pristine, subtly wrong, screaming, and turned inside out.

The fiction it was authored to: stone cut from a volume of space the warp got into. The scorch at the central junction is where something older held that zone — a wound that is also a protection. Ordinary eyes see the burned slab in daylight. A psyker sees what is under it. Same stone, same geometry, two sights.

composited  Both of those come off the same 65,535 × 40,959 master — 2.68 gigapixels, rendered as a grid of tiles straight to a tiled BigTIFF and downsampled here to 1600 px. The burn is painted on after the render: it is the one thing on this page the slab does not contain. Everything else — every vein, speck, fog bank and the entire spectrum change — is the renderer. The unburned masters are at the bottom of this page.

Material Author draws stone as vector geometry, not pixels: veins, specks, fog and warp are a few thousand simple shapes driven by a small recipe. The recipe — a .json slab — is the master, and the render is a deterministic function of it: within one browser build the same slab renders byte-identical every time — the gallery harness gates exactly that, twice from a fresh load — and the same geometry can be drawn at any size. Across browser builds the picture is the same and the bytes are not, because sub-pixel anti-aliasing moves; that comparison is recorded rather than gated. The four steps below came off two slabs, rendered at 1600 × 1000; the post-steps that three of them add are recorded in reference.json, parameter by parameter.

Daylight and psyker are the same slab. One authored geometry, two lights: at rest it's near-true; under the other spectrum the full distortion it was always carrying is exposed.

Then turn the mask inside out. The same disc, in the same place, asked the opposite question: not what did the warp fail to reach but what is the only thing it reached.

The masters, unmarked

pure render  Two slabs under two lights, with nothing added — no burn, no compositing, no retouch. All four were cut on one machine on one afternoon, on the same build, and downsampled to 1600 px from masters of up to 2.68 gigapixels. Each carries its own record of the render that made it — build, engine, hardware class, tile plan, per-tile timings, and a device identity sealed under the slab's own bytes — readable with node tools/tiff-meta.mjs <file>, which is how every figure published about this renderer is checked.

One slab, three materials

pure render  Material Author forks at one seam: a material is a field, a two-dimensional query against that field, and its own control surface. The six plates below come off one slab file carrying one set of geometry — 666 marks, 70 lines, 232 cracks and 8,050 specks, identical in all six of their metadata blocks — cut at 16,384 × 10,240 and downsampled to 1600 px. What that geometry becomes is the material's to decide — and on this slab two of the three show none of it.

Stone — the geometry, drawn

Stone is the material that paints the marks: every vein, crack and speck in the slab is a shape it strokes. Under the psyker spectrum the same geometry answers in emission — all but one vein, which still reads gold.

Glass — a label, not a colour

Glass holds a label where stone holds a colour, so a fold finer than the pixel grid cannot be filtered where it is sampled — it resolves as speckle rather than as a soft edge. Cutting at 16,384 and box-filtering down to 1600 averages 105 samples into every published pixel, which is the same remedy as supersampling and costs one plain render instead of sixteen. It is also the smallest file on this page by a wide margin — 4 MB against stone's 45 at the same size, because smooth bands compress and a speck field does not.

Wood — three cuts of one log

Wood's model is a three-dimensional growth-ring field about a wandering pith. Nothing stores a board — a board is a plane handed to that field, and the cut is decided by where the plane stands: cos θ = plane / r, where r is the radius from the pith at that point. The three below are one log, and they cannot be anything else: the log's seed and scale are deliberately not exposed as controls, so moving the saw plane is the only thing an author can do here. Each is 16,384 × 10,240 in 40 passes of 2048.

What a blur costs

pure render  A master renders as a grid of tiles, and a tile cannot blur what it cannot see. So every blur in the coat makes each tile paint a margin of its neighbours and then throw that margin away. The margin is set by the blur radius, and the coat's radius is a fixed fraction of the picture — at subsurface 2 it is 0.8% of the width — so the bleed grows with every render size while the tile does not.

The pair below is the same picture under the two coat paths, at 65,535 × 40,959 — 2.68 gigapixels, 640 passes of 2048. Only the coat path differs: both carry the same recorded state, and the signature moves only because the renderer is part of it.

32.3× — and it decomposes. 6.3× of it is the extra pixels painted; the remaining 5.1× is that each painted pixel is slower, because the blur runs through ctx.filter and saturates the GPU. It is not a cheaper version of the same thing. The subsurface weights by local contrast; the boost weights by coverage, so it reaches neither soft edges nor the painter's own dither. That is why the fog comes forward and the light mean goes 69.60 → 102.08. Different tool, not a discount — the slower one is kept because it is the one that can do this.

The same comparison, four settings — at 16,384

Four coat settings, each rendered both ways. The first pair is the pair above, cut at a quarter of the width: same recorded signature, and the light means agree to 0.1 across a 4× change of size — 69.70 against 69.60, 102.18 against 102.08. The coat is scaled by the picture rather than by the window, so a smaller render is the same picture, not a crop. At this size the blur costs only 10–15% of the render time, because a 398 px bleed is cheap; the cost lives at size, which is what the 65,535 pair is for.
